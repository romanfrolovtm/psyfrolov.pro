Toxic Relationships: How to Recognise and Leave Them

2026-08-01·3 min read

The term 'toxic relationship' is not a clinical diagnosis, but it describes a real and well-studied dynamic: interaction that systematically depletes, devalues, and constrains one or both partners.

Signs of unhealthy dynamics

Relationship researcher John Gottman identified interaction patterns that most reliably predict relationship breakdown: persistent criticism of a partner's character (rather than specific behaviour), contempt and disdain, defensiveness instead of accountability, and emotional "stonewalling" as a way of avoiding conflict. These four patterns are known in the literature as the "Four Horsemen" of relationship breakdown.

Other signs include constant control, isolation from friends and family, dismissal of a partner's feelings, and cycles of "rupture and reconciliation" without any real change in behaviour.

Why it's hard to leave

Prolonged unhealthy relationships often create a pattern resembling emotional dependency: periods of warmth and attention alternate with periods of devaluation, and it is precisely this unpredictability — not stable closeness — that sustains attachment most strongly. This is not a character flaw; it is a well-documented psychological mechanism known as intermittent reinforcement.

The role of psychological support

Work on this topic typically includes: restoring the ability to recognise one's own boundaries and needs, addressing beliefs such as "I don't deserve better" or "this is my fault", building a plan for safely leaving the relationship if that is the goal, and support during the period after separation.

When it's worth seeking help

There is no need to wait for a situation to become "bad enough". If a relationship consistently triggers anxiety, guilt without a clear cause, or the sense that you constantly have to "brace yourself" around a partner, that is already sufficient reason to seek a consultation.

Practical steps

It helps in practice to keep a brief log of your own state after interacting with a partner: do I feel drained or fulfilled, do I have to "adjust" to the other person's mood, do I feel guilt without a clear reason. Such notes help reveal a dynamic that is hard to assess from inside a relationship, where emotional highs and lows are often perceived as "normal passion". If possible, it helps to discuss these observations with someone outside the relationship — a friend or a specialist — since the pattern is often clearer from the outside.

Questions people often ask

Can toxic relationships be fixed without outside help? Sometimes, if both partners recognise the pattern and are willing to change. But structured work — individual or as a couple — is often needed to shift deeply ingrained habits of interaction.

How do you know it is time to end things rather than work on them? There is no single answer, but a warning sign is when safety (physical or psychological) is at risk, or years of attempts to change the dynamic have not worked.
