How to Choose a Psychologist: What to Look For

2026-08-01·2 min read

Choosing a psychologist is a decision that shapes whether therapy will be useful. Unlike choosing a narrow-specialty doctor, there is no single registry of the 'best', so it helps to know what to look for on your own.

Education and qualifications

The first thing worth checking is basic education. A psychologist typically holds a degree in Psychology (Master's or Specialist level). This differs from a psychiatrist (a medical doctor who can prescribe medication) and from a psychotherapist in the medical sense. A legitimate specialist usually shows their diplomas and continuing-education certificates openly.

Therapeutic approach

Psychology has dozens of orientations — from psychoanalysis to Gestalt therapy. None is "the only correct one", but it's worth understanding which approach a given specialist works in, and why. The cognitive behavioural approach (CBT) stands out for having the largest evidence base of any psychotherapeutic orientation — over 2,000 clinical studies.

Questions worth asking at the first meeting

  • Which approach do you work in, and why that one?
  • What would an approximate plan for my request look like?
  • How do you assess progress?

First impression and safety

Research on the therapeutic alliance — the relationship between client and therapist — consistently shows that the quality of this connection is one of the strongest predictors of therapy success, regardless of the specific approach used. If discomfort persists after the first meeting, that is reason enough to try a different specialist.

Red flags

Be cautious of: promises of fast, guaranteed results; pressure to stay in therapy longer than needed; refusal to explain the working method; judgmental or evaluative comments directed at the client.

Practical steps

In practice, it is worth paying attention not only to the diploma but also to how a specialist talks about their work: do they explain things clearly, without excessive jargon, and are they willing to answer direct questions about their method and experience. It also helps to check whether the stated education is verifiable — real certificates, course dates, institution names. If in doubt, you can ask them to briefly describe how the first session will go and what happens next — an honest answer is usually specific, not vague promises.

Questions people often ask

Does the psychologist's age or gender matter? Research does not show a consistent link between these characteristics and therapy effectiveness. What matters more is the quality of the connection and the trust that forms individually.

What if the first specialist was not the right fit? That is a normal situation. Changing therapists is not a failure — sometimes it takes a few attempts to find the right format of work.
